## The solver path is pure tensor algebra — there's nothing to "implement"

`PKWang.cs` + `MeanField/LocalField` + `FkKernel` decompose into four steps, three of which are one-liners in torch:

1. **`hcum`** — MeanField: sort undirected edge couplings ascending, `cumsum`, scatter back to slots. LocalField: the same *per CSR row*, then symmetrize through the mirror-slot map. Both are `sort` + `cumsum` + `scatter`; the segmented version is a composite `(row, weight)` sort key away.
2. **Bond field over the whole grid** — `g = 1 - exp(-hcum[:, None] / T[None, :])` → one `[E, T]` tensor. That is the entire temperature sweep, evaluated at once.
3. **Landscapes** — `BondMass` and `LocalEnergy` are `index_add_` on `g` and `J*(1-g)` → `[N, T]` in one shot.
4. **Partition** — threshold at θ, connected components. The only non-vectorized piece, and at these sizes `scipy.sparse.csgraph.connected_components` per temperature is milliseconds.

Two things fall out for free that are worth having:

**Exact release temperature.** `g_e(T) > θ ⟺ T < h_e / (-ln(1-θ))`. Each edge's half-bond temperature is closed form — `T_e = h_e / ln 2` at θ=0.5. That's also what `EstimateBracket` is doing when it divides the hcum quantiles by `ln 2`: the bracket is chosen so θ-crossings spread uniformly across the edge population. Node-level τ₅₀(i) needs a scalar solve, but ρᵢ(T) is monotone, so it's interpolation on the `[N,T]` landscape you already have.

**Analytic brittleness.** `−dρᵢ/d log T = (1/dᵢT) Σⱼ hᵢⱼ e^{−hᵢⱼ/T}` — closed form, no finite differencing on a coarse grid.

So `ThermalReleaseFilter` isn't a project. It's maybe 80 lines and it runs on CPU.

## The finding that matters: MeanField SPC *is* single linkage

`hcum` under MeanField is a global sort + cumsum, so `h` is strictly monotone in `J`. Thresholding `g > θ` is therefore identical to thresholding `J > J*(T)`. **The MeanField solver sweep's partition sequence is exactly the bond-percolation / single-linkage filtration on the coupling graph, reparameterized in T.** The cumulative transform only equalizes crossing density along the temperature axis; it doesn't change which partitions appear or in what order.

Two consequences for the note:

- The three-family independence table overstates the SPC/HDBSCAN contrast for that configuration. HDBSCAN *is* single linkage on mutual-reachability plus EOM extraction. Solver-MeanField-SPC and HDBSCAN would be near-redundant instruments, not assumption-diverse ones.
- **LocalField should be the default, not MeanField.** Per-row cumsum makes `hᵢⱼ` depend on ranks within *both* endpoints' neighborhoods, which breaks the global monotone equivalence. That is already a mutual-proximity / local-scaling transform — which is exactly what you want in d≈2048 (see hubness below).

And it sharpens why the MC oracle earns its keep: Swendsen–Wang draws bonds only between *spin-agreeing* pairs on raw `J`, so its co-membership is the random-cluster measure with the q-entropy term — genuinely not bond percolation. Note the two paths also use different energy gauges (solver: `hcum`; MC: raw `J`), so the qualification study must compare *node rank orders and strata*, never temperature values.

## Batched Swendsen–Wang: batch the chains, not the graph

The inner loop is vectorizable except for the union-find. The move is `B = n_temps × n_replicas` independent chains sharing one graph structure:

- `same = spins[:, src] == spins[:, dst]`, `bond = same & (rand([B,E]) < p)` — `p` precomputed `[B, E]`.
- CC via Shiloach–Vishkin hooking + pointer jumping on `[B, N]` parent arrays — `scatter_reduce_(amin)` and `par.gather(1, par)`.
- Recolor in one gather: draw `c [B, N]`, then `spins = c.gather(1, root)`. Every node takes the color drawn at its root.
- All observables (χ_FK, magnetization, energy, co-membership, giant participation) are `scatter_add`/`gather`.

Two design notes that decide whether this is fast or merely correct:

- **Fix the CC round count** to `⌈log₂N⌉+3` instead of looping to convergence. SV converges in O(log N), so it's safe, and it makes the draw a static graph → capturable with CUDA Graphs, which matters because you're launching ~40 tiny kernels per draw and launch overhead dominates.
- **Use a counter-based (Philox-style) RNG keyed on `(chain, draw, edge)`**, not a global generator. That's the torch analogue of `SeedTree.cs`/`SpcSeedHelper`, and it's what makes results invariant to how you chunked the temperature grid — which you will care about the first time you re-run a sweep with a different batch split.

Order of magnitude at N=512, k=15, B=384, 2000 draws: largest tensor per op is ~2M elements, so seconds per layer, minutes for a 24-layer × 5-dose atlas. The MC path stops being a "validation oracle you run occasionally" and becomes affordable everywhere. That weakens the case for the solver being primary — I'd still keep it primary for the *lens* (deterministic, closed-form derivatives), but you can afford MC co-membership as the P7 drift statistic rather than solver affinities.

This is the right thing to be uncertain about, and I think the answer is sharper than "it depends" — there's one constraint that decides most of it, and it's semantic rather than numerical.

## What the Potts formalism actually demands

Four preconditions, in descending order of how hard they bite:

**J_ij ≥ 0.** Non-negotiable. `p = 1 − exp(−J/T)` is a probability. Mixed-sign couplings make it a spin glass: no FK–SW cluster representation, frustration, glacial mixing, and "clusters" that no longer mean communities. Every SPC guarantee evaporates. ThermoMapper enforces the adjacent distinction at the type level — PKWang throws on `EdgeWeightKind.Distance` — and non-negativity comes for free upstream because `GraphCompiler` only ever emits kernel values (Gaussian/Cauchy/Laplacian/Linear).

**Symmetric.** The Hamiltonian has no orientation.

**Sparse and heterogeneous.** The superparamagnetic window only exists if couplings are modularly structured. A dense graph with couplings of similar magnitude goes ordered → disordered with nothing in between. This is *why* Blatt–Wiseman–Domany build kNN graphs — the sparsification isn't incidental, it manufactures the phase you're measuring.

**And the one that actually decides your question: J_ij must mean *similarity*.** Ferromagnetic Potts encodes "i and j want the same label." That's a coherent statement when i and j are observations. When i and j are neurons and J_ij is a weight, a large value means *i drives j* — a flow relation, not a similarity relation. The physics still runs; it just stops corresponding to anything about the computation.

**Weight graphs are flow graphs. Potts models similarity. The overlay type-checks numerically and not semantically.** That's the crux, and it's independent of the sign and density problems (which are also fatal on their own: signed, dense — 2048×5632 ≈ 11.5M edges per MLP matrix, and ×384 chains doesn't fit in memory — bipartite so no within-layer structure, and needing sparsification anyway, which reintroduces exactly the knob you were trying to escape).

## But your instinct is pointing at something real

The attraction of a model-given graph isn't the model-given part per se — it's that `GraphCompiler` has six independent seams (`IDistanceMetric`, `ITopologyGenerator` + k, `ITopologyFilter`, `IMetricRefiner`, `IEdgeScaler` + bandwidth, `ITopologyRepair`), every one a researcher degree of freedom. The note's "freeze graph construction from baseline" guardrail exists *because* those knobs can manufacture the result. A graph the model hands you has none.

So the question worth asking isn't "weights or point cloud," it's **what are the nodes** — because the node set determines which hypothesis you can test, and no pre-existing structure in a transformer has prompts as nodes.

| Graph | Nodes | J ≥ 0? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weight matrix | neurons (bipartite) | signed | No. Flow semantics, dense, wrong node set |
| Attention, post-softmax | tokens in one prompt | **yes, by construction** | **Legitimate.** Different instrument, near-zero knobs |
| Neuron co-activation | neurons | \|corr\| | Legitimate model-side SPC; needs kNN sparsification |
| Causal-patching influence | cases | signed + directed | Right nodes, wrong formalism → magnetic Laplacian |
| Readout-Jacobian / NTK Gram | **cases** | PSD Gram | **The interesting middle ground** |
| Compiled activation cloud | cases | kernel | Primary. Six knobs, but the right node set |

## Where that leaves it

**Primary stays the compiled case graph.** Not because it's better-founded but because H6, P5, and P10 item 8 are all statements about the cohort's relational structure. There is no substitute with the right node set.

**Attention graphs are the one place your overlay idea is physically clean.** Post-softmax weights are strictly positive, naturally concentrated (heterogeneous, which is what SPC wants), and require no metric or bandwidth — you're down to two choices, symmetrization rule and head aggregation, from six. The node set is tokens *within* a prompt, so it answers a different question (does the verdict-relevant token module survive surgery), but it's cheap and it's a knob-free convergence check: if the thermal structure on the compiled graph is real, some shadow of it should survive a construction with nothing to tune. One gotcha — row-stochasticity means the coupling scale goes as 1/n_tokens, so the temperature axis isn't comparable across prompts of different length without normalizing by s_J.

**The middle ground I'd actually push on: keep cases as nodes, take the coupling from the model.** J_ij = alignment of the verdict Jacobians at cases i and j — "do these two cases move the verdict through the same direction" — or the empirical-NTK Gram, "do they recruit the same parameters." Right node set, PSD by construction, and it replaces *the metric choice* with a model-given inner product while leaving only kNN sparsification as a knob. That's your instinct with the semantics intact. Same circularity caveat as before: Jacobian-derived couplings can't carry the native-vs-imposed comparison, only P7 collateral.

**Neuron co-activation is the honest model-side SPC** — nodes = neurons, J = |corr| over the cohort, kNN-sparsified. Similarity semantics (unlike weights), and it's the classic functional-connectivity construction SPC was built for. It answers "does surgery reorganize the model's functional modules," which is a real complement to the case-side question and lands closest to H8 and P9.

One aside on the deepest reading of your question — "can we use the model's own thermodynamics." The model does have an energy function, but it's the modern-Hopfield energy that attention implements, not a Potts model on weights. That's a different (and interesting) line, and it's not SPC.

If you want the knob-free control, attention graphs are cheap enough to prototype standalone. If you want the version that serves the programme's hypotheses, it's the Jacobian-coupled case graph.

`FisherRaoSimplex` uses `2·arccos(BC)`, the radius-2 embedding, so **d_FR ∈ [0, π] always**. And `Wasserstein1` is the CDF-difference form `Σ|cumP − cumQ|` — which means it's *already* W₁ with ground metric `|i−j|` on the index. For attention over token positions that's exactly the right ground cost, and it's O(n), not even O(n log n), because the support arrives sorted.

## The argument for a geometry prior here is narrower and stronger than "fidelity"

Your six-knob problem doesn't actually go away by picking a better metric. But one specific knob does, and it's the one the programme's guardrail depends on.

The note requires freezing bandwidth from baseline and reusing it across layers, doses, and prompts. Under Euclidean that freeze is **nominal** — attention entries scale as 1/n, so ‖α_i − α_j‖ shrinks like 1/√n, and a σ fit on short prompts is simply the wrong σ on long ones. The frozen bandwidth isn't controlling anything; it's drifting.

Under Fisher–Rao the diameter is π. Fixed. Independent of n, of layer, of dose. A bandwidth in FR units means the same thing everywhere, so freezing it is a real control. Same for W₁ once you divide by (n−1) to land in [0,1].

**That's the actual case for the geometry prior: it converts a nominal control into a real one.** Fidelity to the simplex is the reason it works, but the payoff is methodological.

Concretely, with d ∈ [0, π] and a Gaussian kernel, σ=0.5 spans ~10⁹ in coupling, σ=1.0 spans ~140×, σ=2.0 spans 3.4× — flat enough that the superparamagnetic window closes. So σ ∈ [0.5, 1.0] is the sane band and you can *say that in advance*, which you cannot do in Euclidean units.

## What each candidate actually buys

**Fisher–Rao.** Canonical — it's the geometry *of* the object, not a hypothesis about it. Cheap (one sqrt, one dot, one arccos, fully batched). Bounded. One real caveat: the √ transform is variance-stabilizing, so it *upweights differences in small probabilities*. For attention, the small probabilities are largely softmax floor — you'd be letting noise into the metric. Worth a pre-registered floor (drop mass below some ε and renormalize) rather than discovering it as an artifact.

**Wasserstein.** This is the only candidate that knows the sequence is a sequence. Under FR and Euclidean alike, attending to token 20 versus token 21 is maximally different — zero credit for near-misses. W₁-positional gives graded credit for how far mass has to slide. Given that guard prompts have strong positional structure (template scaffold, then the case, then the verdict slot), that's not a refinement, it's a different and probably more faithful notion of "these two tokens attend similarly."

On W₂ specifically: you named it and I'd substitute W₁ only knowingly. In 1-D both are closed form — W₁ from CDFs (shipped), W₂ from quantile functions (~15 more lines, a merge of the two CDF step functions). The difference is how hard you penalize long-range transport: W₂'s quadratic cost makes attention *jumps* count much more than local smearing. If you think a token attending 200 positions away versus 5 away is a categorical difference rather than a graded one, W₂ is the right call. That's a substantive modeling choice, not a convenience one.

The expensive version — ground metric = distance between key vectors, so "these tokens attend to semantically similar things" — needs Sinkhorn and goes quadratic in pairs. Real, but not first.

**Hyperbolic.** I'd push back, constructively. Your intuition is presumably hub structure: scaffold tokens absorb enormous mass, hub-and-spoke reads as tree-like. That pattern is genuinely there in guard prompts. But there's no canonical map Δ^{n−1} → hyperboloid — you'd be *fitting an embedding*, which adds researcher freedom rather than removing it. That's backwards from what you want out of a geometry prior.

And you already have the test. P8 is exactly the "is this hierarchical enough to earn hyperbolic" instrument: Gromov δ plus matched-dimension distortion. Build the cloud under Fisher–Rao, run δ on it, and let the existing machinery decide. If δ comes back small, the hyperbolic arm is *earned* and P8 applies unchanged. Costs nothing extra because it's already scheduled.

## The kernel is a separate knob and it controls something specific

Gaussian `exp(−d²/2σ²)` → light tail, strong locality. Laplacian `exp(−d/σ)` → heavier. Cauchy `1/(1+d²/σ²)` → polynomial. For SPC this isn't cosmetic: heavy tails mean many weak-but-nonzero couplings, which drives global ordering at lower T and can close the superparamagnetic window entirely. **Gaussian is the conservative default** and it's ThermoMapper's default in `GlobalBandwidthScaler` for that reason. Save Cauchy for the sensitivity grid.

## What's n-dependent and what isn't

| Construction | Scales with n? | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Euclidean on rows | yes, ~1/√n | none clean — this is the problem |
| `A Aᵀ` (co-attention) | yes, ~1/n | note this *is* Euclidean+linear, the naive default in disguise |
| Fisher–Rao | no — diameter π | none needed |
| W₁ positional | yes, range [0, n−1] | divide by (n−1) |
| Raw `A_ij` as coupling | yes, s_J ~ 1/n | the τ = T/s_J normalization already in the note |

Worth seeing that `(A Aᵀ)_ij = ⟨α_i, α_j⟩` — the obvious co-attention matrix is precisely Euclidean metric with linear kernel. So this isn't "principled geometry versus the simple thing." It's three points on one axis, same node set, directly comparable.

## What I'd pre-register

Three arms on the token node set: `A Aᵀ` (Euclidean + linear, the baseline you have to beat), Gaussian-on-Fisher–Rao with σ ∈ [0.5, 1.0], and Gaussian-on-W₁-positional normalized to [0,1]. Then δ on the FR cloud to decide whether a hyperbolic arm opens. Route (A) — raw `A_ij` as the coupling, zero metric, zero kernel — stays alongside as the knob-free control.

One implementation gotcha: `arccos` has infinite derivative at 1, and near-identical attention rows (adjacent tokens, very common) push BC to 1−ε. ThermoMapper clamps to avoid NaN but that doesn't recover the lost precision. Hellinger — `2√(1−BC)`, the chordal distance on the same sphere — is monotone in d_FR, so it gives **identical kNN neighbor ordering** with much better conditioning. Use FR for reported distances, Hellinger for graph construction, and you lose nothing.

Checked `JSDistance.cs` — it already returns `sqrt(0.5·(KL(p‖m)+KL(q‖m)))` in nats, so it *is* √JS, range [0, √ln2 ≈ 0.833].

**I'd take √JS over Fisher–Rao here.** It fixes the specific objection I raised against FR and costs you one thing.

## Why it's the better divergence arm

**It kills the floor-noise problem.** That was my one real caveat against FR, and it's not marginal. FR depends on BC = Σ√(p_k q_k), so a coordinate sitting at the softmax floor contributes with sensitivity ∂√p/∂p = 1/(2√p) — unbounded as p→0. Perturb a floor entry ε→2ε and BC moves like √ε. JS's contribution from the same coordinate is ½[ε ln(2ε/q) + q ln 2], which moves like ε·log(1/ε). At a floor around 1e-6 that's one to two orders of magnitude less sensitive. The mixture m = (p+q)/2 is doing the smoothing for free, which is exactly the pre-registered floor-and-renormalize hack I was going to make you write.

**It's still a true metric and still bounded**, so the whole frozen-bandwidth argument survives intact. Diameter √ln2 rather than π. I'd divide by √ln2 (or just use log base 2) so the arm lands in [0,1] and its σ is directly comparable to normalized-W₁'s — otherwise you're eyeballing bandwidths across two different unit systems. In nats the sane Gaussian band is roughly σ ∈ [0.15, 0.35]; normalized, [0.18, 0.42].

**It's genuinely Hilbertian, and geodesic Fisher–Rao isn't.** JS is conditionally negative definite (Fuglede–Topsøe), so by Schoenberg `exp(−JS/σ²)` — the Gaussian kernel on the √JS metric — is positive definite. The Gaussian kernel on *geodesic* FR is not PD, because that's spherical geodesic distance. This matters downstream: your coupling matrix is then a real Gram matrix, so the Fiedler filter, algebraic connectivity, and effective resistance all behave without indefinite-kernel workarounds, and it stays consistent with the PSD assumptions in `bayesian_kernel_projection.py` / LEACE / whitened SVD.

**The interpretation fits attention better.** JS = H(m) − ½(H(p)+H(q)) — the entropy you gain by averaging two attention patterns. Equivalently, the mutual information between token-identity and attended-position for a uniform two-token mixture: *how identifiable is the source token from one sample of where it looks*. That's a more natural reading for this data than "angle between square-rooted attention rows."

## The cost: you lose the GEMM

Hellinger/FR all-pairs is **one matmul** — BC = (√P)(√P)ᵀ, then `arccos`. √JS has no such factorization, because m depends on the pair. The entropy decomposition halves it (H(p), H(q) precompute per row; only H(m) is pairwise), but you still need the [n, n, d] mixture. At n=512 that's ~540MB fp32 — chunk it and it's nothing. At n=2048 it's ~34GB and chunking is mandatory. Manageable, but it's a real difference if you ever want per-head-per-layer all-pairs across a big cohort.

## What it does *not* fix — and what that implies for the arm set

√JS saturates on disjoint support at ln2, exactly as FR saturates at π. Attention is concentrated, so near-disjoint row pairs are common, and you'll get a pile of distances pinned at the ceiling with no gradation among them. Pick one — √JS — and spend the arm budget on W₁, which is orthogonal to both: non-saturating, positionally aware, sensitive precisely where the divergence metrics go flat.

- **Gaussian on √JS/√ln2**, σ ∈ [0.18, 0.42] — divergence geometry

# What about FR half-plane vs angular?

They're not two versions of one metric — they're Fisher–Rao for two **different statistical models**, so choosing between them is choosing what an attention row *is*.

`FisherRaoHalfPlane` takes `[mu, log_sigma]` and returns `√2·arccosh(1 + (Δμ² + 2Δσ²)/(4σ₁σ₂))` — the FR distance for the **univariate Gaussian family**. Hyperbolic, curvature −1/2, unbounded. `FisherRaoSimplex` is FR for the **categorical family** — spherical, radius 2, diameter π. Same principle, opposite curvature, because they're distances between different parametric models of the same data.

For attention rows that cashes out as:

- **Simplex/angular**: the row is a categorical distribution over token *identities*. Permute the positions and the distance is unchanged — position-blind. That's precisely the near-miss deficiency I flagged, stated as a property of the model rather than a quirk of the formula.
- **Half-plane**: the row is a Gaussian over *position*. Position-aware by construction. 512 dims → 2.

## Your hyperbolic intuition was right, just about the wrong object

This is the canonical route to hyperbolic geometry here, and it answers my earlier objection. The half-plane isn't hyperbolic because you embedded something into it — it's hyperbolic because the Fisher metric of the normal family *is* hyperbolic. No embedding to fit, no distortion to measure, no extra freedom introduced. If you wanted hyperbolic on principled grounds rather than as a fitted hypothesis, this is where it lives.

## Two reasons it still can't be the metric

**Multimodality is annihilated, and attention is routinely multimodal.** Sink mass on BOS, a local window, a few content tokens. The (μ,σ) summary of a bimodal pattern puts the centroid *between* the modes, where there is no mass at all. That's not lossy, it's misleading — two tokens with entirely different bimodal patterns can land on identical (μ,σ).

**Concentration would dominate location.** Run the numbers on the formula: two diffuse tokens (σ≈50) whose attention centroids differ by 100 positions give z=2, d≈1.86. A sink token (σ≈1) against a diffuse one (σ≈50) with *identical* centroids gives z=25, d≈5.5. The σ mismatch beats a 100-position location difference by about 3×. So the metric would mostly be measuring how peaked each token's attention is, not where it goes.

Related: it's unbounded — d diverges like √2·ln(1/σ) as σ→0, and attention sinks are near-delta. So you'd need a σ floor, which is a new and consequential knob, and you lose the bounded-diameter property that motivated this whole line of reasoning.

## Where it does earn a place

**As a Mapper lens, not a graph metric.** A lens doesn't have to be a complete summary — it has to be a meaningful coordinate to pull the space apart along, and (μ_i, σ_i) is a genuinely interpretable pair: *how far back does this token look, and how diffusely*. Multimodality wrecking the metric doesn't disqualify it as a coordinate. `MultiD.cs` handles 2-D covers, and the half-plane metric gives you a principled way to define overlap in the right geometry instead of as a Euclidean product of intervals. The σ→0 divergence flips from bug to feature there — it isolates the attention sinks automatically.

**And it's the correct metric for a different object you already have**: bootstrap distributions of estimated quantities. Comparing "estimate ± uncertainty" across layers or doses in half-plane geometry is more principled than comparing means and SDs separately — it correctly treats a gap of 0.1 between two tight estimates as larger than the same gap between two diffuse ones. Cheap, small, and it's the natural home for the metric.

## Why it doesn't earn an arm

On the axis where it would have helped — positional awareness — it's a two-moment lossy version of W₁. Nearly dominated, with one genuine exception: the hyperbolic curvature encodes "the same positional shift means more between two sharp heads than between two diffuse ones," which W₁ treats linearly. If that weighting turns out to be what you want, the cleaner move is to normalize W₁ by pooled spread rather than to swap geometries.

So the arm set holds: √JS (robust, position-blind) + W₁ (positional, non-saturating) + `A Aᵀ` baseline. Half-plane enters as a lens.

One disambiguation in case you meant the other "angular": plain cosine on *raw* rows (`Cosine.cs`) isn't FR-anything — it's the ambient angle without the variance-stabilizing √, and it's what `A Aᵀ` reduces to under row normalization. Already in the baseline; no reason to add it separately.

# would this new solver even really be "superparamagnetic" anymore? its based on static analysis of Potts model, its a combination of graph compilation and some thermodynamics, no sampler involved. It feels like it is "neither beast nor man" metaphorically

Your instinct is right that something's off, but I think it's pointing backwards rather than forwards.

## "Superparamagnetic" names a phase, not an algorithm

In a physical superparamagnet, ferromagnetic grains sit in a non-magnetic matrix: below each grain's ordering temperature but above the global one, every grain is internally aligned while the grains are mutually decorrelated. Net magnetization zero, subsystems ordered. Blatt–Wiseman–Domany borrowed the name because that's exactly what a clustered Potts model does — within-cluster co-membership near 1, between-cluster co-membership at chance.

That is a property of the **Gibbs measure of the Potts model on your graph**. Swendsen–Wang is one *estimator* of that measure. Belief propagation is another — BP fixed points are stationary points of the Bethe free energy, which is a variational approximation to the same Gibbs free energy, exact on trees. Neither one *is* the phase.

So the sampler was never what made it superparamagnetic. Sampler-free equilibrium statistical mechanics is the norm, not an exception: Onsager's exact 2-D Ising solution, transfer matrices, series expansions, the cavity method, the whole RG apparatus. Nobody says a transfer-matrix calculation of the Ising critical point isn't about the Ising critical point.

## The inversion: PKWang was the one that wasn't superparamagnetic

This is the part worth sitting with. Drop `q^C` and you don't just lose an entropic correction — you lose **q entirely**. And without colors there is no chance-level co-membership.

Watch what the current solver's co-membership does across the sweep: `g = 1 − exp(−h/T)` runs from 1 at low T to **0** at high T. Not to 1/q. To zero. There is no chance floor, because there is nothing to be at chance about.

But the superparamagnetic phase is *defined* by the coexistence of two things: within-domain order, and between-domain correlation **at the chance level 1/q**. Remove the chance level and the phase has no definition. The system goes from one giant component to N singletons with nothing in between except the single-linkage dendrogram — which is precisely what it does.

**So the name was already unearned, and not because of the missing sampler.** BP is what restores the thing that earns it. The successor is more superparamagnetic than its predecessor, not less.

That also gives you a concrete acceptance test rather than a naming argument. Keep the name only if you can show a temperature window where the pair marginals have block structure with within-block ≫ 1/q and between-block ≈ 1/q, stable across the window. If BP gives a smooth crossover with no intermediate regime, the phase isn't there and the name goes. That's falsifiable, it's computable from BP's own output, and the old solver fails it by construction.

## What genuinely does change

Not the physics — the epistemics, and it's worth writing into the methodology because it changes the error decomposition the note prescribes.

With SW you get co-membership as an empirical frequency: it carries **Monte Carlo error, which you shrink by running longer**, and the note's guardrail correctly separates that from data uncertainty via the outer bootstrap. With BP you get it as a computed number with *no* stochastic error but an **approximation bias you cannot shrink at all** — only bound by comparison. So the decomposition goes from (MC error + data uncertainty) to (Bethe bias + data uncertainty), and the first term is estimable only against the oracle.

That makes the oracle's job specific rather than decorative. BP is exact on trees, and kNN graphs are triangle-dense; Bethe approximations on loopy ferromagnets typically overestimate order and place transitions too high. So the qualification study isn't "do the node rankings agree" — it's **does SW co-membership show the same block structure over the same normalized temperature window**. If BP invents a superparamagnetic window that SW doesn't see, that's the Bethe bias showing up as a phase.

## Naming

I'd keep "superparamagnetic" on the phenomenon and qualify the estimator: *superparamagnetic clustering, cavity estimator*. Same convention as everywhere else in the field — you don't rename critical exponents when you switch from Monte Carlo to series expansion. Reserving "SPC" for the SW-sampled BWD construction is fine as a narrower historical usage, but the phase name belongs to the phase.

## On "mostly graph compilation"

That worry is more legitimate than the sampler one, and it gets sharper if you move the Lorenz normalization upstream as I suggested — then the compiler fixes the coupling structure before any thermodynamics runs.

But it's an empirical question, not a definitional one, and you already have the test shape from the original note: the thermal construction has to beat weighted degree. Generalize it. The compiler determines *which relations exist*; the thermodynamics determines *which of them cohere, and at what scale*. Those are separable, and the second's marginal contribution is measurable — swap the BP marginals for a monotone function of the couplings and see whether the block structure, the lineages, and the nerve survive. If they do, the thermodynamics is decoration and you've learned that cheaply.

"Neither beast nor man" is the right feeling about the *old* solver, which had the vocabulary of one and the mathematics of the other. The BP version is just the beast, computed differently.

# yea i mean a fine point here is while merging functionalities and sharing them, i also need to retain interfaces that capture the domain-specific semantics so that a user of HDBSCAN can think in HDBSCAN terms and a user of SPC can think about SPC. the backend captures the common abstraction and the api interfaces capture the domain semantics on either side of a given shared core abstract

Agreed — and I'd sharpen one thing: **the facades shouldn't be symmetric.** They should differ in what they're allowed to promise, and that asymmetry is information rather than an inconsistency to iron out.

The nestedness case is the example already in hand. HDBSCAN's condensed tree is nested by construction — it's built from an MST, so it can't not be. SPC's thermal stack isn't. So the shared core has to be the general object (filtration), and then HDBSCAN's facade can expose `Dendrogram` *unconditionally* while SPC's exposes the filtration and offers the tree bridge only when `NestingHolds`. Same core, different guarantees, and each facade tells its user the truth about their algorithm. Forcing both to expose the same surface would either weaken HDBSCAN's contract or launder SPC's.

## The translation table is also the documentation

| Core | HDBSCAN facade | SPC facade |
|---|---|---|
| filtration parameter | λ = 1/d_mreach | T, gauged as τ = T/s_J |
| edge scalar → merge height | mutual reachability | release temperature |
| filtration | condensed tree *(nested: guaranteed)* | thermal stack *(not guaranteed)* |
| persistence bar | stability (mass-weighted λ span) | lineage persistence |
| antichain extraction | EOM | thermal EOM / plateau |
| unassigned | noise (−1) | periphery / peripheral capture |
| per-point landscape | λ_birth/λ_death | bond mass, giant participation |
| null / chance level | — | 1/q |
| soft assignment | membership probability | co-membership marginal |

Two asymmetries worth designing around rather than smoothing over. **HDBSCAN has no null** — its parameter is a distance, and distances have no chance level — so the shared "threshold with null correction" takes an optional null and HDBSCAN's facade passes none. And **SPC's soft assignment is a pair statistic** (co-membership between i and j) where HDBSCAN's is a point statistic (probability that i belongs to its cluster). Those aren't the same shape, so the core wants both slots and each facade fills one.

Both parameters happen to refine as they increase, which is a genuine convenience — the core can have a canonical direction and neither facade has to invert.

## The core has to speak a third vocabulary

If the core says "temperature," SPC's facade becomes a pass-through and the core will quietly accrete thermal assumptions. If it says "lambda," the reverse. Neutral terms — filtration parameter, merge height, persistence, antichain, gauge — cost more up front and are what keep both facades honest, because neither can be accidentally free.

GMM is the test for whether you actually achieved neutrality. Its merge tree is parameterized by Bhattacharyya or entropy or BIC, and if that facade comes out awkward, the core was never neutral — it was SPC's vocabulary with the labels filed off.

## The failure mode to design against

Shared-core-plus-facades degrades when a facade re-implements a piece of the core to fix something for its domain. The invariant that prevents it: **a facade may contain translation and assertion, never algorithm.**

Concrete test — after the merge, delete a facade and reproduce its output by calling the core with the right parameters. If you can't, algorithm has leaked upward. Practically that means `SingleLinkage.FromCouplingGraph` should end up around five lines over `FromEdgeScalar`, and `ThermalDendrogram.FromEdgeCurves` likewise. The moment either grows a branch, the branch belongs below.

## Your tree is already the facade structure

`graphical/hdbscan/`, `graphical/spc/`, `statistical/gmm/` are the facades; `primitives/` and `dendrograms/` are the core. So this is a downward movement of shared logic, not a reorganization — the shape is already right. And `HdbscanSession` / `SpcClusteringSession` are the API anchors that should keep their vocabulary hardest, since they're what a user actually holds.

## Where the two genuinely converge — and it's the compiler

Right now HDBSCAN builds its own graph and SPC is handed one, which looks like a permanent domain difference. It isn't.

Mutual reachability is `max(core_k(a), core_k(b), d(a,b))` — a per-edge transform using node-level quantities. That's exactly what `IMetricRefiner` does, and `PathNeighborRefiner` is the existing example. `CoreDistances` is already in `Graphs.Primitives.Mst`, i.e. already on the shared side.

So `MutualReachabilityRefiner` slots into the compiler pipeline, HDBSCAN consumes a compiled graph like everything else, and it reduces to **compile → single linkage → EOM** — the shared spine exactly, with its facade reduced to nearly pure vocabulary. That's the strongest argument for doing the compiler overhaul first: it's not just prerequisite infrastructure, it's where the HDBSCAN/SPC asymmetry actually dissolves.

# You're beginning to see why all of this is such a tangle, with grand triangulation questions between different thermomapping clustering modules, graph compiler, maths primitives, TDA engine. and another beef i have with my graph compiler right now is its semantics. I really would don't want to express "MutualReachabilityRefiner" I want to express parameters and graph native, domain agnostic operations as knobs that compose into results like "refinement that ensures mutual reachability"

You already have exactly one instance of the pattern you want, and everything else should be made to look like it:

```
EdgeFieldSymmetrization.Combine(a, b, rule)   // rule ∈ {Min, Max, Mean}
```

That's a graph-native, domain-agnostic operation with the semantics in the knob. `MutualKnnFilter`, `LocalMutualProximityScaler`, `PathNeighborRefiner`, `AutoLmpScaler` are all recipes wearing the type system.

## The verbs, and the proof that they're enough

Decompose the two recipes that matter and they come out of the same three verbs:

**Mutual reachability** — `max(core_k(i), core_k(j), d_ij)`
```
nodeReduce(dist, orderStat(k))  →  lift(max)  →  combine(dist, max)
```

**Local scaling** — `exp(−d²/(σ_i σ_j))`, σ = k-th neighbour distance
```
nodeReduce(dist, orderStat(k))  →  lift(product)  →  combine(dist², ratio)  →  map(gaussian)
```

Identical shape; three different op arguments. Two classes become one composition with different knobs — and that's the actual test of whether the algebra is real.

**Lorenz/hcum** needs one more verb, because it produces per-edge output from a node's incident set rather than a scalar:
```
nodeScan(coupling, cumsumAscending)
```

So the vocabulary is roughly: `generate` · `nodeReduce` (sum/mean/max/order-stat/quantile/entropy) · `nodeScan` (rank/cumsum/z-score/softmax) · `lift` (min/max/mean/product/geomean) · `combine` · `symmetrize` · `map` (kernel + bandwidth) · `filter` (predicate on a field) · `repair`.

Note that `nodeReduce` and `nodeScan` occupy the same slot and distinguish two whole families: local scaling is a reduce (normalize by a scale), mutual proximity is a scan (normalize by a rank). That distinction is currently invisible because both are just "scalers."

## The deeper semantics problem: edge fields are untyped

I think this is the real source of the tangle. `double[] weights`, `double[] g`, `double[] hcum`, `double[] delta` are the same type and mean completely different things — a distance, a coupling, a bond probability, a cumulative mass, a rank. Nothing prevents feeding any of them anywhere.

`EdgeWeightKind` already exists as the beginning of the fix, but it's enforced ad hoc: PKWang throws at the consumer if handed a `Distance`-weighted graph, and that's the only place it's checked. Promote it into the op algebra and every op declares what it consumes and emits:

```
gaussian kernel     : Distance    → Coupling
nodeScan(cumsum)    : Coupling    → Mass
FkKernel            : Coupling × T → Probability
lift(max)           : NodeScalar  → same kind as source
threshold(θ, null)  : Probability → Partition
```

Then composition is self-checking, the semantics are legible from the signature, and the recipe names become derivable rather than asserted.

And it would have caught the PKWang gauge mismatch mechanically. `hcum` is a **Mass**; SW feeds `FkKernel` a **Coupling**. The two paths were running the bond kernel on different kinds of quantity, which is exactly why solver-T and MC-T were never commensurable — a type error that the current `double[]` regime cannot express, let alone reject.

## Keep the names — demote them from classes to values

The cost of a knob algebra is the blank page: a user who wants mutual reachability now has to know the composition. That's what the named classes were buying, and it's worth keeping.

The resolution is that a recipe becomes a *value*, not a type:

```
Recipes.MutualReachability(k)   // returns the composition
```

Same discoverability, but transparent — you can print it, diff it, and edit one op without subclassing. And it serializes into `GraphCompilerConfig` as **the operations that ran** rather than a class name, which means two runs are comparable at the level of what was computed. That's the same argument as naming the θ null: the knob being explicit is what makes it auditable. "Frozen graph construction from baseline" becomes a checkable claim rather than a nominal one.

## The ordering constraint dissolves, and something better replaces it

The five interfaces have five signatures, which is what forces the fixed generate → filter → refine → scale → repair order. A uniform op type removes that — and some legitimate pipelines genuinely want a different order (scale before filter; refine after repair).

But the fixed order also prevents nonsense, so don't just drop it. Replace it with **declared preconditions** validated at composition time: this op requires a coupling-kind field, or a symmetric field, or a connected graph. That's strictly better than positional ordering — it permits valid reorderings and rejects invalid ones, where the current scheme does neither.

## One caveat on scope

A knob algebra is easy to over-build. The honest threshold is roughly three recipes sharing three primitives, and you're past it — mutual reachability, local scaling, mutual proximity, and Lorenz all share `nodeReduce`/`nodeScan` + `lift` + `combine`, and the same verbs recur across HDBSCAN's core distances, SPC's coupling prep, and the Mapper filters. That justifies the algebra. It would not justify generalizing `generate` or `repair`, which really are small closed sets — leave those as they are.

## The correction that makes the problem smaller

The chain you wrote — CND → PD over couplings → valid for SPC — has a false link in the middle, and cutting it unblocks metrics you might otherwise reject.

**PD does not imply entrywise non-negative.** A positive-definite kernel can have negative entries. And **SPC doesn't need PD** — the Potts Hamiltonian needs `W ≥ 0` and symmetric, nothing more.

So PD is neither necessary nor sufficient for SPC-validity. Practically: for any exp-family kernel the entries land in (0,1] automatically, so **non-negativity is free regardless of the metric**. You can run SPC on geodesic Fisher–Rao, or on the registry's spherical-geodesic "cosine," with a Gaussian kernel, and the model is perfectly well-posed. PD-ness only bites for consumers that read W as a Gram matrix.

Three orthogonal axes, three consumer classes:

| Requirement | Who actually needs it | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| `W ≥ 0`, symmetric | **SPC/Potts**; graph Laplacian ops — Fiedler, algebraic connectivity, effective resistance | free for exp-family kernels |
| `W` is a Gram matrix (PD) | kernel PCA, MDS embedding, anything treating `W` as an inner product | `d²` CND (Schoenberg) |
| curvature / injectivity radius | manifold estimators — Fréchet/Karcher mean uniqueness, manifold median, mxPBF | Karcher–Kendall conditions |

Worth stating the first row explicitly because it's routinely conflated: `xᵀLx = ½ Σ W_ij (x_i − x_j)²`, so `L` is PSD whenever `W ≥ 0`. Spectral graph methods sit on row one, not row two.

## The PD axis is a table of citable facts, not logic

Schoenberg gives the rule — `exp(−t·d²)` is PD for all t iff `d²` is CND — and the entries are known results, not things to derive at runtime:

| Metric | `d²` CND | `d` CND | Admissible exp kernel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Euclidean | ✓ | ✓ | Gaussian, Laplacian |
| Hellinger (chordal) | ✓ | ✓ | Gaussian, Laplacian |
| √JS | ✓ (Fuglede–Topsøe) | ✓ | Gaussian, Laplacian |
| W₁ / L¹ | ✗ | ✓ | **Laplacian only** |
| spherical geodesic (= FR simplex, = registry `cosine`) | ✗ | ✗ | neither is PD |
| hyperbolic geodesic (Poincaré/Lorentz) | ✗ | ✓ (negative type) | **Laplacian only** |

Two things fall out of that table.

First, a correction to what I told you earlier: I recommended **Gaussian** on W₁/(n−1) for the positional arm. That's wrong on the PD axis — L¹ is of negative type but its square isn't CND, so the **Laplacian kernel** is the admissible one there. Same for anything hyperbolic. The SPC arm is unaffected (non-negativity is what it needs), but any consumer reading those couplings as a Gram matrix would be on unsound footing.

Second, there's a pattern worth encoding rather than memorizing: **for negative-type-but-not-Hilbert metrics, the Laplacian kernel is admissible where the Gaussian isn't.** That turns `Kernels.{Gaussian, Cauchy, Laplacian, Linear}` from a taste menu into a computed set — `admissibleKernels(metric.Properties, consumer)` — which is precisely the "knobs compose into results" shape you're after. The composition checker doesn't encode the math; it looks it up.

## "Fisher–Rao" names nothing admissibility-relevant

FR on the simplex is spherical. FR on (μ, σ) is hyperbolic, K = −1/2. FR on SPD covariances is non-positively curved. One construction, three curvatures, three different admissible kernel sets.

So the properties attach to the **(family, metric) pair**, never to the metric's name. That's the strongest concrete argument for the table-as-data approach: a name-keyed lookup would be actively wrong here, and the two FR entries in `maths/distance/geodesic/` are the proof sitting in your own tree.

## Curvature is declared in two places

`MetricProperties.SpaceGeometry` and the `IRiemannianManifold` zoo both know the geometry, separately — `FisherRaoSimplex` ↔ `SphericalManifold`, `FisherRaoHalfPlane` ↔ `PoincareBallManifold`/`LorentzHyperboloidManifold`. Same shape as the dendrogram split: one concept, two homes.

And `SpaceGeometry` is already the weaker declaration — three values, no way to express Grassmann, Stiefel, product, or scaled manifolds that the zoo already has. So the manifold should be the authority and `MetricProperties` should reference it rather than re-declare a coarser version. The admissibility layer is where those two finally have to meet, which is another reason it's the right place to do this work rather than a nice-to-have.

## One gap worth knowing about

`MetricRegistry` exposes six metrics; `maths/distance/` has roughly fourteen. **`FisherRaoSimplex`, `Wasserstein1`, and `JSDistance` aren't in the registry at all** — they exist but aren't reachable from the compiler. So none of the attention arms we sketched can currently be expressed through `GraphCompilerConfig`, which is worth knowing before you plan around them.

And `MetricKind.Cosine` dispatches to `SphericalGeodesicMetric`. That's a live Gaussian-not-PD case in the shipped default set — harmless for SPC, unsound for anything treating those couplings as a Gram.
