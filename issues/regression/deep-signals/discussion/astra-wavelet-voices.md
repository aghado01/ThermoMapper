Wavelets have been used successfully for speaker discrimination. The choice of mother wavelet is only part of what made them work. Researchers have also varied which frequency bands receive fine resolution, how much timing detail survives, and which measurements they combine across a stretch of speech. For your project, I’d pose the question this way:
What multiscale acoustic features let us recognize a returning speaker after their words, pitch, or recording conditions change, and how short a segment supplies enough evidence?

That captures something specific about your experience as a listener. In a two-person conversation, you can often hear that the first voice has returned, even if you have never heard either person before. That is a within-conversation matching problem. It gives you a more accessible first target than finding a globally unique code for every voice. Perception studies also show why the distinction matters: unfamiliar listeners can hear differences readily, yet sometimes sort variable recordings of one person into several perceived identities. Lavan et al., How many voices did you hear?

Your STFT intuition points to two structures visible in the same voiced sound. The spacing between harmonic lines tells you the fundamental frequency, even when the lowest line is weak. The heights of those lines trace a broader spectral envelope shaped by the excitation and vocal-tract resonances. In the synthetic figure, read each row left to right. Across the top, pitch changes while the envelope stays fixed. Across the bottom, the pitch stays fixed while the envelope changes.

A speaker can produce both kinds of change: raising their pitch changes harmonic spacing, and moving from “ee” to “ah” changes the resonances. That makes a single spectral snapshot a poor candidate for a permanent barcode. A better candidate is a pattern of recurring relationships across sounds. For example, given comparable vowels, how do the resonance bands sit relative to one another? As pitch rises, how does energy redistribute among harmonics? Which of those relationships recur across several turns? In controlled listening experiments, Gaudrain and colleagues found that listeners tolerated a larger proportional change in glottal pulse rate than in apparent vocal-tract length before judging that the speaker had changed. Gaudrain et al., The Role of Glottal Pulse Rate and Vocal Tract Length in the Perception of Speaker Identity

Two lines of wavelet research speak directly to this:
- Wavelet packets tested on speaker verification. Siafarikas, Ganchev and Fakotakis used a Battle–Lemarié polynomial spline wavelet, a direct connection to your spline work. In a related study, they estimated how informative eight frequency regions were and designed a packet tree that gave different regions different resolution. They tested it on the male portion of the 2001 NIST speaker evaluation. Their work gives you a concrete historical candidate and, more importantly, a way of choosing the frequency partition using discrimination performance. Its results are specific to that evaluation. Wavelet Packet Based Speaker Verification · Objective Wavelet Packet Features for Speaker Verification
- Wavelet scattering tied to sound production. Lostanlen and Mallat analyze a harmonic source–filter model using wavelets along time, log-frequency, and octave. Their result relates the scattering coefficients to the motion of pitch and spectral envelope. This is the closer theoretical match to your intuition: it asks what a coefficient means when a harmonic sound changes. Speaker discrimination is a further property one could investigate using those coefficients. Wavelet Scattering on the Pitch Spiral
I’d choose the wavelet by first specifying the distinction it must expose. If you want to follow individual harmonic ridges, a densely sampled complex Morlet CWT is a reasonable starting point. At frequency \(f\), compare its approximate bandwidth \(f/Q\) with the harmonic spacing \(F_0\). A band much wider than \(F_0\) blends neighboring harmonics. A narrower band resolves them better but averages over a longer interval, which matters when pitch moves. If you want the broader envelope, some blending is desirable. A wavelet packet tree offers a different control: you can subdivide a frequency region more finely wherever that improves speaker comparison. Both choices make the analysis scale explicit.

For your theory-first approach, I’d start with a crossed comparison: same speaker at different pitches and on different vowels; different speakers at matched pitch and vowel. Ask which wavelet measurements still distinguish the second pair while keeping the first recognizably related. Then cross the microphones too. Two excerpts from the same recording share a channel, and an unsupervised encoder can learn that shortcut remarkably well. Lepage et al., Self-Supervised Positive Sampling for Robust Self-Supervised Speaker Verification

That gives “barcode” a testable meaning for your generative prior: a latent inferred from one short turn should help predict the speaker-dependent acoustic structure of a later turn, even when the later turn contains different speech. You can then ask which wavelet measurements supply that predictive gain, how quickly it accumulates, and under what conditions two voices remain ambiguous.
